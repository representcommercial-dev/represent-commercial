Buying your current business premises

Can you buy the commercial property your business already rents?

Potentially. The owner must be willing to sell, and familiarity with the building does not remove the need to test the price, property, funding and ownership structure. The opportunity should be assessed as both a business-premises decision and a commercial property purchase.

The short answer

Occupancy knowledge helps. It is not due diligence.

Operating from the property gives you useful knowledge about access, layout, services, neighbours and day-to-day constraints. It may also reduce relocation disruption. But it can create false confidence. A long-term tenant may know how the building works without knowing its title, approvals, structural condition, flood exposure, contamination risk, capital expenditure or market value.

The decision begins with a simple question: if your business did not already occupy this property, would you still choose to buy it at the proposed price?

01

Will the owner sell?

A direct approach can test interest before a property is marketed. The approach should protect confidentiality and avoid revealing urgency or a maximum budget.

02

What is a defensible price?

Comparable sales, vacant-possession value, the lease position, building condition and required works all affect the price a buyer should support.

03

Does ownership fit the business?

Test deposit and transaction costs, debt service, outgoings, capital works and the capital the property would tie up against the benefit of control.

04

Will the property remain flexible?

Consider whether the premises can support growth and whether another occupier would lease or buy it if the business later moves.

A practical purchase pathway

1. Review the lease and timing

Check the option date, expiry, notice requirements, rent reviews, make-good obligations and any purchase right or first-refusal clause. The lease position affects leverage and the time available to fund, investigate and settle a purchase.

2. Confirm purchase readiness

Before approaching the owner, establish an indicative budget, funding capacity and likely buyer entity with your broker, accountant and other qualified advisers. A business should not create a sale opportunity it cannot execute.

3. Approach the owner and test the price

The approach can be made directly or through a buyer-side adviser. If the owner is open to selling, assess the asking position against market evidence rather than treating avoided relocation costs as a reason to overpay.

4. Investigate the property

Coordinate legal, building, planning, environmental and other investigations appropriate to the asset. Familiarity with the premises does not replace specialist reports or contract protections.

5. Negotiate the contract and transition

Price is only one term. Deposit, due-diligence conditions, finance, GST treatment, settlement timing, existing lease treatment and any required works must be documented with your solicitor.

Questions to resolve first

Owner: Who owns the property, and are they genuinely open to selling?

Price: What do comparable transactions support?

Fit: Will the premises still suit the business in five or ten years?

Capital: What cash is required for deposit, duty, GST treatment, advice and works?

Structure: Which entity should buy, subject to legal, tax, SMSF and finance advice?

Exit: Could the property be leased or sold if the business later relocates?

Common questions

Buying from your landlord

Can I ask my landlord to sell me the commercial property?

Yes. A business can make a direct or adviser-led approach even if the property is not advertised for sale. The owner is not obliged to sell, so the approach should establish interest without weakening the buyer's negotiating position.

How do I know what the property is worth?

Price should be tested against comparable sales, the property's condition, zoning and constraints, its value with vacant possession or the current lease, and the cost of any works the business needs. A formal valuation may also be required by the lender.

What happens to the existing lease if I buy the property?

That depends on the buyer entity, the lease and the contract. The lease may continue, be assigned or require another documented arrangement. Your solicitor should advise on the correct treatment before the contract becomes unconditional.
