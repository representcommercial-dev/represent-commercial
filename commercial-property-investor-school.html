Commercial Property Investor School

Understand the income. See the potential.

Five short, interactive lessons for investors assessing a commercial property acquisition or value-add opportunity in Queensland. Test the income, the cost of the plan, the leasing risk, the debt and the downside before you commit.

A commercial property is bought for its income. Buying it right means knowing what that income is really worth, what it will cost to improve, how long it takes to lease, how debt changes the outcome and what happens when an assumption is wrong.

Each lesson asks one practical question, lets you change the inputs and shows the result, the formula and what the model leaves out. The examples are illustrative. They show how the numbers move, not what a particular property will return.

Yield, income and value

The decisionWhat is this income worth, and how much does that answer depend on the capitalisation rate you assume?

What changed: at 6.00%, every $1 of net income supports about $16.67 of value. Moving the cap rate by one percentage point, from 5% to 7%, moves the indicative value from $3,000,000 to $2,142,857 on the same income.

Formula

Indicative value = annual net operating income ÷ capitalisation rate

$150,000 ÷ 6% = $2,500,000

Net operating income is rent and recoveries actually receivable, less operating expenses the owner pays and cannot recover.

Not included

  • Purchase costs, duty and GST.
  • Lease expiry, vacancy, incentives and capital works.
  • Rental reversion, where passing rent sits above or below market.
  • Debt, tax and holding period.

What to understand

A cap rate is a market judgement about risk and income quality. It should be supported by comparable sales of similar assets with similar leases, tenants and condition, adjusted for the differences.

A cap-rate assumption is not a valuation. A registered valuer considers the lease terms, tenant covenant, building, location, market evidence and other methods before forming an opinion of value.

Small cap rate changes move value a long way. The lower the cap rate, the more sensitive value becomes.

Due diligence questions

  1. Is the net income sustainable after removing one-off income, above-market rent and expenses the owner is paying but has not disclosed?
  2. Which recent sales support the cap rate, and how do their leases, tenants and buildings compare?
  3. How much income is at risk from lease expiries or vacancy in the next three years?

Not included

  • Sale costs, tax and GST.
  • Time to stabilise and the risk of delay.
  • Any change in cap rate caused by a different lease profile.

What to understand

A value-add plan creates value only when yield on total cost is above the cap rate the market will apply to the stabilised income. Here 6.21% against 6.00% leaves a thin margin, and the stress test shows how quickly it disappears.

Value above cost is an estimated, unrealised figure. It becomes cash only if the property is sold, after selling costs and tax. Refinancing against it creates debt, not profit.

Energy and operating savings increase the owner’s net income only if the owner keeps them. Where outgoings are recovered from tenants under the lease, savings flow to the tenants instead.

Due diligence questions

  1. What signed leases or tenant enquiries support the rent the plan relies on, and which incentives will the market expect?
  2. Have the works been priced by someone who will do them, with approvals, contingency and the income lost during works included?
  3. What does the plan look like if the uplift is achieved a year later than expected?

Formula

Paying months = window − vacancy − rent-free months (not below zero)

Base rent in window = annual rent ÷ 12 × paying months

A: $100,000 ÷ 12 × 34 = $283,333

B: $115,000 ÷ 12 × 27 = $258,750

Not included

  • Outgoings recovered from the tenant, and outgoings the owner carries while the space is vacant.
  • Leasing fees, fit-out and other incentives beyond rent-free time.
  • Rent reviews, discounting and the time value of money.
  • The value effect of a longer or stronger lease.

What to understand

This compares base rent cash only, over the same period for both options. It is not a full effective-rent analysis or a valuation.

A delayed lease also runs beyond the end of the window. Option B may still have months to run when option A ends, which this comparison does not capture. A full assessment also weighs lease term, tenant covenant, review structure and the market evidence for each rent.

For an investor, this is why an achievable leasing plan matters before purchase. Vacancy assumed away in the price becomes a cost after settlement.

Due diligence questions

  1. What signed leases, not asking rents, support the higher rent, and how long did comparable space take to lease?
  2. What incentives are tenants in this precinct receiving on new leases?
  3. What outgoings will you carry while the space is vacant?

What changed: a 65% loan on $3,000,000 is $1,950,000. After repaying $1,500,000 and $10,000 of costs, $440,000 could be released. The property then carries more debt and higher interest.

Formula

New loan = value × new LVR

Cash release = new loan − existing loan − refinance costs

Equity remaining = value − new loan

$3,000,000 × 65% = $1,950,000; $1,950,000 − $1,500,000 − $10,000 = $440,000

Not included

  • Lender serviceability, interest cover and credit assessment.
  • The lender’s own valuation, which may differ from your estimate.
  • Interest on the larger loan and its effect on cash flow.
  • Tax on how released funds are used.

What to understand

A negative release means the new loan does not cover the existing debt and costs, so cash must be contributed to refinance.

Borrowed money is not profit. A release increases debt, interest and risk, and the property still has to service the loan through vacancy and rate changes. Lesson 5 tests that.

The LVR here is an assumption. The amount a lender offers depends on its valuation, the income, the borrower and its policy at the time. This lesson is not a borrowing recommendation.

Due diligence questions

  1. What value will a lender’s valuer support, based on signed leases rather than projected income?
  2. Can the stabilised income cover interest on the larger loan at a higher rate?
  3. What will the released funds be used for, and have you discussed the structure with your accountant and financial adviser?

Formula

Income received = NOI × (12 − vacant months) ÷ 12

Cash flow before tax = income received − outgoings carried while vacant − owner-retained costs − capex allowance − interest − principal

Interest cover = (income received − vacant outgoings − owner-retained costs) ÷ interest

Principal and interest uses a fixed monthly payment over the term. The first 12 monthly payments are split into interest and principal.

Not included

  • Income tax, depreciation and GST.
  • Rent reviews, cost growth and rate changes after month 12.
  • Lender covenants, fees and serviceability tests.
  • Sale, revaluation and capital growth.

What to understand

This is a simplified first-year view, before tax. It is an illustration of how the inputs interact, not a forecast or a loan offer.

Principal repayments are cash out of the property, but they are a repayment of debt, not an operating expense. They are shown separately from interest so you can see both.

Net operating income is already after owner-paid operating expenses. Only enter costs below that are not already deducted, so nothing is counted twice.

Due diligence questions

  1. How many months of vacancy and outgoings could you fund from outside the property?
  2. What does the loan look like at the rate you could face at the next refinance, not today’s rate?
  3. Which capital items, such as roof, air conditioning or compliance works, are due in the first five years?

Investor acquisition checklist

Seven questions to answer before you commit to a commercial property investment.

  1. Sustainable net incomeRent actually receivable, less owner-paid outgoings, with one-off income and above-market rent removed.
  2. Comparable signed leasesEvidence of signed rents and incentives on similar space nearby, not asking rents.
  3. Lease and covenant qualityLease term, expiry profile, review structure, security, tenant financial strength and options.
  4. Building, history and complianceCondition, historical use, contamination risk, approvals, fire and building compliance, and capital items due.
  5. All-in cost and liquidityPrice, duty, due diligence, finance and plan costs, plus the cash you keep after settlement.
  6. Downside sensitivityCash flow and loan to value under vacancy, higher interest rates and a softer cap rate.
  7. Execution after settlementWho will lease, manage and deliver the plan, on what timeline and at what cost.
