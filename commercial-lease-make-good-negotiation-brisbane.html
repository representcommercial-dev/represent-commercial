Make-good

Commercial lease make-good negotiation for Brisbane and SEQ tenants.

Make-good is the cost of leaving, and it belongs in a premises decision long before the lease ends. This guide sets out how I would approach it for a business tenant, and where a solicitor or building specialist is needed.

Nick O'TooleFounder & Director, Represent Commercial. Draft prepared 16 November 2026; publication date to be confirmed
Direct answer

Can a tenant negotiate make-good?

Often there is something to discuss, but the starting point is what the lease requires. First establish the obligation, with your solicitor. Then work out what the landlord is actually seeking, what it would cost, and which practical options exist, such as carrying out agreed works or agreeing a payment instead.

No outcome is guaranteed. The lease, the condition of the premises, the landlord's plans and the timing all shape what can be agreed.

What make-good means in a premises decision

A make-good clause sets out how the tenant must return the premises at the end of the lease. The Queensland Small Business Commissioner describes it as the tenant's obligation to return the property to its original condition, or as specified in the lease (QSBC, Make good).

In practice that can mean removing fit-out, repairing damage and restoring finishes or services. The cost sits with the business at the point it is trying to move, so it changes the comparison between renewing and relocating. The renew or relocate guide covers how make-good fits into that comparison.

Understand it before expiry, relocation or a new lease

Make-good is easiest to plan for when there is still time. Before the expiry, before committing to new premises, and before signing a new lease, the business should know roughly what it may be asked to do and what that could cost. The QSBC notes that not completing make-good obligations on time may result in additional rent or penalties (QSBC, Make good).

When negotiating a new lease, the make-good terms are part of the deal. Agreeing a clear scope and benchmark condition at the start can avoid a dispute at the end.

The legal obligation and the commercial outcome are different questions

What the lease requires is a legal question for your solicitor. The QSBC notes that the Property Law Act 2023 includes standard terms for commercial leases, including a term about returning the premises in the condition they were in at the start, subject to reasonable wear and tear and other specified exceptions. The parties can agree to exclude or replace the standard terms, so the lease itself needs to be checked. For retail shop leases, the QSBC also notes that under section 50B of the Retail Shop Leases Act 1994 a clause requiring the tenant to refurbish or refit is void unless the lease includes general details of the nature, extent and timing of the requirements (QSBC, Make good). How any of this applies to a particular lease is for your solicitor to advise.

The commercial question is different: once the obligation is understood, what is the most sensible way to resolve it? That is where I can help, alongside your solicitor rather than in place of them.

Condition records, photographs and documents

Evidence of the condition at the start of the lease matters. The QSBC suggests making an entry condition report or taking photos or video when the lease begins, and keeping copies of the lease, bond or guarantee and correspondence (QSBC, Return of a commercial lease bond).

Useful documents include the lease and any variations, the disclosure statement for a retail shop lease, fit-out approvals and drawings, any condition report or schedule, photographs at handover, and correspondence about works or repairs during the tenancy.

Identify the works or payment being sought

Ask the landlord to set out in writing what it requires: the works, the standard it expects, and whether it would consider a payment instead of works. A clear schedule makes it possible to test each item against the lease and against the condition records.

Independent scopes and cost evidence

A make-good figure is only as reliable as the scope behind it. Where the amounts are material, an independent building consultant or quantity surveyor can scope and cost the works. Compare that against any landlord estimate on the same basis, item by item.

Represent Commercial does not provide building, quantity surveying or valuation advice. Those specialists are engaged by the tenant where needed.

Timing, access and handover

Physical works need time, trades and access, and they can disrupt a business that is still operating or moving. Plan the programme against the expiry date, the move to new premises and any period of overlap. Agree in writing how and when the premises will be inspected and handed back.

Comparing practical options

Depending on the lease and the landlord, options may include carrying out the works, carrying out an agreed reduced scope, or agreeing a cash settlement instead of physically making good. The QSBC lists cash settlement as one option tenants may have (QSBC, Make good). Where a renewal or new lease is being negotiated with the same landlord, make-good may also form part of those wider terms.

Compare each option on total cost, time and risk to the business. None is available as of right, and the landlord may prefer works it can control.

How I can help, and when you need a specialist

Having spent more than 20 years on the owner's side of commercial property, I understand how landlords look at make-good. When appointed through commercial tenant representation, I can help assess the commercial position, compare the options and negotiate the commercial terms, alongside your solicitor and any building consultant you engage.

  1. Solicitor. What the lease requires, whether a clause is enforceable, and any dispute process.
  2. Building consultant or quantity surveyor. The scope of works and a cost estimate.
  3. Mediation. The QSBC can mediate make-good disputes up to $750,000 in value. Retail shop lease disputes not resolved at mediation can go to QCAT, and courts consider disputes for non-retail leases (QSBC, Make good).

Make-good also affects the cost of moving. Use the Lease Renewal vs Relocation Calculator or the Occupancy Cost Comparison to see it alongside rent, fit-out and relocation costs.
