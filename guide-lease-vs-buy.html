Lease vs buy for the owner-occupier

Most businesses ask which is cheaper. That is the wrong question.

Nick O'Toole Principal & Licensee, Represent Commercial

Every owner-occupier I speak to opens with the same framing: is it cheaper to rent this place or to own it? Run the numbers on year one and leasing almost always wins. Rent is smaller than a mortgage repayment, there is no deposit, and no stamp duty. If cost in the first year is the test, stop reading and keep leasing.

It isn't the test. The right question is how long you intend to occupy the premises, because that single input decides the answer. Buying carries a large cost at the front — deposit, duty, due diligence — and pays it back slowly through equity. Leasing carries no entry cost and returns nothing. Somewhere on the timeline the two cross. Your job is to work out whether your holding period sits before that point or after it.

What each option actually costs

Leasing costs you rent, outgoings and, at the end, make-good. It is a clean operating expense with no residual. Buying costs you the deposit and acquisition costs up front, then loan repayments, then the outgoings a landlord would otherwise carry — structural repairs, the roof, the sinking fund. In exchange you accumulate equity, and the asset either holds or grows in value.

The comparison only works if you count both sides. Buying without crediting equity overstates its cost. Leasing without crediting the deposit you did not spend overstates its virtue: that capital, left in the business or invested, earns something. Both figures below are net positions, not cash costs.

The crossover, and what moves it

On these assumptions the lines cross in year four. Before that, the duty and acquisition costs have not been recovered and leasing is genuinely the better financial outcome. After it, the gap widens every year — by year ten buying is roughly $400,000 ahead, and almost all of that difference is equity you own rather than rent you have paid to somebody else.

Three inputs move the crossover, and only one of them is in your control. Capital growth is the largest: at 3% the crossover falls in year four, and if the asset is flat it pushes out past year seven. The interest rate matters less than people expect, because principal repayment converts to equity regardless. What you control is the holding period — and that is why the decision is a business question about how long you will be in these premises, not a spreadsheet question.

If you know you'll be there in ten years, buy. If you might not be there in three, lease. The middle is where the analysis earns its keep.

Rent paid to your own entity

Most owner-occupiers buy the premises in a separate entity — a trust, a company, or a self-managed super fund — and have the trading business lease it back at market rent. The trading business claims the rent, and the rent lands in an entity you control. It is the same cash leaving the business either way, but it stops leaving your balance sheet.

The rent has to be genuine market rent, documented in a real lease. Setting it artificially high to shift profit invites scrutiny; setting it low starves the owning entity. This is the single most common thing done casually and it should be structured properly at the start, with your accountant.

Holding it in an SMSF

Business real property is one of the few assets a self-managed super fund may acquire from, and lease to, a related party. That makes it a common home for owner-occupied premises. The rules are strict and worth knowing before you get attached to the idea:

  • The property must be wholly and exclusively used in a business, so mixed residential use is generally out.
  • The lease to your business must be on arm's-length commercial terms and the rent must actually be paid, on time, every time.
  • Borrowing inside the fund happens through a limited recourse borrowing arrangement, which lenders price above ordinary commercial debt and cap at lower leverage.
  • Contribution caps set the pace at which the fund can accumulate the deposit, so the timeline is often longer than the business would like.

Handled well, it is an efficient structure. Handled casually, it creates compliance problems that cost more than the rent ever saved. Get advice specific to your fund before committing.

Exit and resale

The exit is where owning quietly outperforms, and also where it can disappoint. An owner-occupied building sold vacant is worth less than the same building sold with a lease on it, because an investor is buying income. If you are selling and moving on, put your own business on a proper lease first and sell the asset with a tenant in place. It is the same building; it prices as a different product.

Bear in mind that a specialised fitout you built for your own operation rarely adds value for the next occupant. Buy for the location, the access and the shed, not for what you intend to do inside it.

What I would advise

If your business is established, your premises requirement is stable, and you can fund the deposit without straining working capital, buy. Ten years of rent buys somebody else an asset. The example above is not unusual — it is close to the median outcome in Brisbane industrial over a normal holding period, and the advantage compounds.

If you are growing fast, unsure of your space requirement, or the deposit would come out of stock and equipment, keep leasing and revisit in two years. Buying the wrong building is a worse outcome than renting the right one. And be honest about the deposit: if your business genuinely returns 20% on capital, that capital may be better left in the business than committed to a deposit. But compare the right things. The 3% in this example is capital growth on the property, not a return on your money — on a 70% loan, 3% growth on $1.8M is $54,000 of value against $540,000 of equity, so the equity itself compounds at closer to 10% before repayments are counted. Leverage is what makes the comparison closer than it first looks.
