Lease vs buy

Should your business lease or buy its commercial premises?

For an owner-occupier, this is not a comparison between rent and mortgage repayments. It is a decision about capital, occupancy cost, flexibility and what happens when the business eventually needs to move. This guide sets out how I would think it through.

Nick O'TooleFounder & Director, Represent Commercial
Direct answer

Is it better to lease or buy commercial premises?

There is no universal answer. Buying can give the business control over its premises, exposure to the property's long-term value and a way to direct occupancy payments towards an asset the owner controls. Leasing preserves capital, keeps the business flexible and avoids tying money to a building that may stop suiting the business.

Buying tends to suit an established business with a stable space requirement, a long expected holding period and a deposit that does not strain working capital. Leasing tends to suit a business that is growing quickly, is unsure of its future requirement or is better served by keeping capital in the business.

The question is not simply: “Is buying cheaper than leasing?”

It is: “Which option produces the better commercial outcome for this business?”

Why rent versus mortgage is the wrong comparison

Leasing and buying are two different uses of capital. A tenant pays rent for the right to occupy and keeps its capital in the business. An owner commits a deposit and acquisition costs, takes on debt and carries costs a landlord would otherwise carry, but builds equity and keeps exposure to the property's future value.

The useful comparison is total occupancy cost against the capital each option consumes. Both sides need crediting. Buying without counting the equity it builds overstates its cost. Leasing without counting what the unspent deposit could earn overstates its advantage.

What changes the answer?

The crossover is not fixed. Three inputs move it most, and only one of them is in the business's control.

Capital growth

This has the largest influence. At 3% a year the example crosses in year four. If the property's value is flat, the crossover moves out past year seven. Growth should never be treated as guaranteed.

Holding period

This is the input the business controls. Duty and acquisition costs are paid up front, so a short occupation rarely recovers them. The longer the business expects to stay, the more the ownership economics matter.

Interest rates and equity

Rates matter less than most people expect, because principal repayments convert to equity regardless. The size of the deposit matters more, because it sets both the borrowing cost and the capital no longer available to the business.

Purchase price, market rent, ownership costs and transaction costs also move the result. A model helps structure the decision. It cannot make it without understanding the business.

What else could the capital do?

A deposit committed to premises cannot also fund stock, equipment, hiring or growth. A purchase can be commercially sensible and still be the wrong capital-allocation decision for a particular business.

Compare the right figures. If the business genuinely returns 20% on capital, that capital may be better left in the business. But the 3% in the example is growth in the property's value, not the return on the money invested. On a 70% loan, 3% growth on $1.8M is $54,000 of value against $540,000 of equity, so the equity itself grows at closer to 10% before repayments are counted. Leverage is what makes the comparison closer than it first looks.

Does the property work for the business, and for how long?

The financial model is irrelevant if the building stops working for the business. Location, size, configuration, access, parking, loading, power and room to grow all need to suit the operation now and over the expected holding period.

Flexibility is the main thing ownership gives up. A tenant can relocate at lease expiry. An owner who needs to move must sell, or find a tenant and become a landlord. A business that is growing fast or unsure of its space requirement should give that loss of flexibility real weight.

If leasing looks stronger, compare premises options on total cost with the Occupancy Cost Comparison, and consider whether tenant representation would help secure the right premises and lease terms.

Paying rent to your own entity

Many owner-occupiers buy the premises in a separate entity, such as a trust, company or self-managed super fund, and have the trading business lease it back. The rent must be genuine market rent, documented in a proper lease. Setting it too high or too low creates problems for one entity or the other.

Business real property is one of the few assets an SMSF may acquire from, and lease to, a related party. The rules are strict. The property must be used wholly and exclusively in a business, the lease must be on arm's-length terms and the rent must be paid on time. Borrowing inside the fund uses a limited recourse arrangement, usually at higher cost and lower leverage, and contribution caps limit how quickly a deposit can be built.

Structure should be settled with your accountant, solicitor and financial adviser before the contract is signed. Represent Commercial provides commercial property advice, not legal, tax, financial or structuring advice.

What happens when you eventually sell?

A property that suits the business today may one day need to be sold, leased to another occupier or adapted. That exit should be considered before buying.

Two buyer pools matter. Investors buy income, so a proper lease to your own business opens the property to them. Owner-occupiers buy premises and often pay more for vacant possession. Selling with or without your business as the tenant can price the same building as two different products.

Specialisation narrows both pools. A fitout built for one operation rarely adds value for the next. Before buying, ask who would want the property later, whether it could readily be leased, whether it could suit a different user and what capital expenditure it may need. Buy for the location, the access and the building, not for what you intend to do inside it.

What I would look at

Before recommending that an owner-occupier buy rather than keep leasing, I would want answers to these questions.

  1. How long will the business actually stay? A short occupation makes transaction costs and lost flexibility harder to justify.
  2. Does the property work operationally? Size, configuration, access, loading, power and room to grow.
  3. How much capital does it consume? And what would that capital otherwise do in the business?
  4. What happens if the business outgrows it? Whether it can be expanded, adapted, leased or sold.
  5. Who will want it later? Future tenant and buyer demand matter at acquisition.
  6. What capital expenditure is coming? Roof, structure, services and plant, not only the settlement figure.

If the business is established, the space requirement is stable and the deposit can be funded without straining working capital, buying is often the stronger path. If the business is growing fast, is unsure of its requirement or would fund the deposit from stock and equipment, keep leasing and revisit the question in two years.

Common questions

Is it better to lease or buy commercial premises?

There is no universal answer. Buying can provide control over the premises, exposure to long-term value and equity. Leasing preserves capital and flexibility. The right decision depends on the business, the property, the finance structure, the expected holding period and what the capital could otherwise do.

When should a business buy its premises?

Buying usually makes most sense when the business is established, its space requirement is stable, it expects to stay for many years and it can fund the deposit and acquisition costs without straining working capital.

How long do you need to own for buying to beat leasing?

It depends on the assumptions. In the illustrative Acacia Ridge example, buying overtakes leasing in year four at 3% annual growth, and after year seven if values are flat. Because duty and acquisition costs are paid up front, short holding periods rarely favour buying.

Can a self-managed super fund buy business premises?

An SMSF may acquire business real property from, and lease it to, a related party, subject to strict rules including arm's-length lease terms and exclusive business use. Specialist legal, tax and financial advice is required before proceeding.
