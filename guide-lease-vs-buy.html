Lease vs buy

Should your business lease or buy its commercial premises?

For an owner-occupier, the lease-versus-buy decision is not simply a comparison between rent and mortgage repayments.

It involves capital allocation, occupancy costs, flexibility, finance, property ownership, operational requirements and what happens when the business eventually needs to move.

This guide shows how I would think about the decision.

Direct answer

Is it better to lease or buy commercial premises?

There is no universal answer.

Buying can provide greater control over the premises, exposure to the property's long-term value and the ability to direct occupancy payments towards an asset the business owner controls.

Leasing can preserve capital, provide greater flexibility and reduce the amount of money tied to a property that may eventually stop meeting the business's requirements.

The appropriate decision depends on the business, the property, finance structure, expected holding period and what the capital could otherwise be used for.

The right question is not simply: “Is buying cheaper than leasing?”

It is: “Which option produces the better commercial outcome for this business?”

Commercial context

Why is lease vs buy complicated?

The comparison involves two different uses of capital.

When leasing, the business generally preserves more capital but pays rent for the right to occupy premises.

When buying, the business commits capital to a property and takes on ownership costs and financing obligations, but may also build equity and retain exposure to the property's future value.

The comparison therefore needs to consider more than the monthly payment.

The crossover

The crossover, and what it means

The economics of leasing and buying can change over the holding period. A purchase usually requires substantially more capital upfront, so leasing may look economically attractive early in the comparison.

Over a longer period, ownership may begin to benefit from equity accumulation and changes in property value. Under a particular set of assumptions, the relative economic position can cross. But the crossover is not fixed.

Buy

Net position if the unit were bought now and sold at that anniversary, after selling costs.

Lease

Crossover

Where buying's net position overtakes leasing's. On these inputs, year four.

Time and measure

Key insightThe important question is not “When does buying always beat leasing?” It is “Under these assumptions, when does the economic relationship change?”

On these assumptions the lines cross in year four. Before that, the duty and acquisition costs have not been recovered and leasing is genuinely the better financial outcome. After it, the gap widens every year. By year ten buying is roughly $400,000 ahead, and almost all of that difference is equity you own rather than rent you have paid to somebody else.

Three inputs move the crossover, and only one of them is in your control. Capital growth is the largest: at 3% the crossover falls in year four, and if the asset is flat it pushes out past year seven. The interest rate matters less than people expect, because principal repayment converts to equity regardless. What you control is the holding period, and that is why the decision is a business question about how long you will be in these premises, not a spreadsheet question.

Ownership structure

What does it mean to pay rent to your own entity?

Most owner-occupiers buy the premises in a separate entity (a trust, a company or a self-managed super fund) and have the trading business lease it back at market rent. The trading business claims the rent, and the rent lands in an entity you control. It is the same cash leaving the business either way, but it stops leaving your balance sheet.

The rent has to be genuine market rent, documented in a real lease. Setting it artificially high to shift profit invites scrutiny; setting it low starves the owning entity. This is the single most common thing done casually and it should be structured properly at the start, with your accountant.

The commercial concept

  • The operating business and the property-owning entity may be separate.
  • Commercial rent may still be relevant between them.
  • Ownership structure can affect asset protection, finance, taxation and succession considerations.
  • These matters require appropriate specialist advice.

Represent Commercial provides commercial property advice. It does not provide legal, tax, financial or structuring advice. Speak with your solicitor, accountant, financial adviser and tax adviser before deciding on a structure.

Superannuation

Holding it in an SMSF

Some business owners consider holding commercial premises through a self-managed superannuation fund. This can materially change the structure of the transaction and the relationship between the operating business and the property owner. It is not appropriate for every buyer.

Business real property is one of the few assets a self-managed super fund may acquire from, and lease to, a related party. That makes it a common home for owner-occupied premises. It is not appropriate for every owner-occupier, but it shows how much the ownership structure can change the property decision. The rules are strict and worth knowing before you get attached to the idea:

01

The property must be wholly and exclusively used in a business, so mixed residential use is generally out.

02

The lease to your business must be on arm's-length commercial terms and the rent must actually be paid, on time, every time.

03

Borrowing inside the fund happens through a limited recourse borrowing arrangement, which lenders price above ordinary commercial debt and cap at lower leverage.

04

Contribution caps set the pace at which the fund can accumulate the deposit, so the timeline is often longer than the business would like.

Handled well, it is an efficient structure. Handled casually, it creates compliance problems that cost more than the rent ever saved. Get advice specific to your fund before committing.

The property decision needs to be coordinated with appropriately qualified advisers before acquisition.

Key principleProperty structure should be considered before the contract is signed, not discovered afterwards.

Exit and resale

What happens when you eventually sell?

An owner-occupier should consider the eventual exit before buying. A property that perfectly suits the business today may eventually need to be sold, leased to another occupier or adapted to another use.

The exit is where owning quietly outperforms, and also where it can disappoint. Whether the building is worth more sold vacant or with a lease on it depends on who is buying at the time. Investors are buying income, so a proper lease to your own business opens the property to them. Owner-occupiers are buying premises, and recently they have often paid more than investors for vacant possession. If you are selling and moving on, test both routes before deciding whether to sell the building vacant or with your business in place as the tenant. It is the same building; it can price as a different product.

Bear in mind that a specialised fitout you built for your own operation rarely adds value for the next occupant. Buy for the location, the access and the shed, not for what you intend to do inside it.

WHAT I WOULD look at.

Before recommending that an owner-occupier buy rather than continue leasing, I would want to understand more than the purchase price and rent comparison.

How long will the business actually stay?

If the business is likely to move again relatively soon, the transaction costs and inflexibility of ownership become more important.

Does the property actually work for the business?

Location, size, configuration, access, parking, loading, power, warehouse requirements, office requirements and future growth.

How much capital does the purchase consume?

The acquisition should be considered against other uses for that capital.

What could that capital do in the business?

A property acquisition can be commercially sensible while still being the wrong capital-allocation decision for a particular business.

What happens if the business outgrows it?

Whether the property can expand, be adapted, be leased or be sold.

Who will want the property later?

Future tenant and purchaser demand matter at acquisition.

What capital expenditure could be coming?

A buyer should understand likely future works rather than focusing only on settlement.

Is the property decision distorting the business decision?

Do not buy the wrong property simply because ownership itself is attractive.

I would want the property decision to support the business, not force the business to work around the property.

What I would advise

If your business is established, your premises requirement is stable, and you can fund the deposit without straining working capital, buy.

If you are growing fast, unsure of your space requirement, or the deposit would come out of stock and equipment, keep leasing and revisit in two years. Buying the wrong building is a worse outcome than renting the right one. And be honest about the deposit: if your business genuinely returns 20% on capital, that capital may be better left in the business than committed to a deposit. But compare the right things. The 3% in this example is capital growth on the property, not a return on your money. On a 70% loan, 3% growth on $1.8M is $54,000 of value against $540,000 of equity, so the equity itself compounds at closer to 10% before repayments are counted. Leverage is what makes the comparison closer than it first looks.
