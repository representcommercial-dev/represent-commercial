Direct answer

How do I avoid buying the wrong commercial property?

Understand what you are acquiring before you commit capital. For a tenanted investment, that means the property, the tenant, the lease, the income, the outgoings and what happens if the income stops. For an owner-occupier, it also means whether the building works for the business and who would want it later.

A headline yield or current rent does not explain those risks. Each needs evidence, and anything that cannot yet be verified should be treated as an unknown rather than an assumption.

The question is not only: “What does this property yield today?”

It is: “What happens to me financially if the assumptions change?”

The central idea

You're buying more than the building.

When you acquire a tenanted commercial investment, you're not simply buying land and improvements.

You're also acquiring an income stream whose quality can depend on the tenant, lease, property, market and the owner's future obligations.

A headline yield or current rent does not explain all of those risks.

Before buying, a purchaser should understand what could happen if the assumptions supporting the investment change.

Vacancy

WHAT HAPPENS IF THE PROPERTY SITS VACANT FOR NINE months?

Nine months is not a normal or expected vacancy period. It is a downside scenario worth understanding before you buy, because vacancy can affect more than rent.

Potential consequences can include:

  • Loss of rental income
  • Owner-funded outgoings
  • Advertising costs
  • Leasing commission
  • Incentives for a new tenant
  • Works required before reletting
  • Finance commitments continuing without rental income
  • Pressure on the owner's cash reserves

The question isn't simply, “What does this property yield today?” It is also: “What happens to me financially if that income stops?”

Tenant risk

What happens if the tenant fails?

A lease is only as useful as the tenant's ability and willingness to perform its obligations.

Before acquiring a tenanted commercial investment, the buyer should understand the tenant and the lease supporting the income.

Covenant quality

Covenant quality is essentially the strength and reliability of the tenant and the lease supporting the property's income.

It is a judgement drawn from several factors. It cannot be reduced to a single score.

Relevant considerations can include

  • Tenant entity
  • Trading history where available
  • Guarantees
  • Security arrangements
  • Lease term remaining
  • Options
  • Rent reviews
  • Arrears or disputes where disclosed
  • Permitted use
  • Concentration of income
  • Reletting prospects if the tenant leaves
The lease

Do you actually understand the lease you're buying?

Commercial leases can materially affect investment performance, and they work very differently from residential tenancies. For buyers moving from residential property, this is often where the biggest gaps sit.

Represent Commercial provides commercial property acquisition advice, not legal advice. Appropriate lease and contract documentation should be reviewed by the buyer's solicitor. Our role is to identify commercial issues and help the buyer understand what should be investigated with the appropriate advisers.

Consider

  • Remaining lease term
  • Options
  • Rent review mechanism
  • Recoverable outgoings
  • Incentives
  • Guarantees and security
  • Repair and maintenance obligations
  • Make-good provisions
  • Assignment provisions
  • Permitted use
  • Responsibilities at lease expiry
Outgoings

Who actually pays the outgoings?

“Net lease” does not automatically mean every property expense will always be recoverable from the tenant. Lease wording and circumstances matter.

The buyer needs to understand

  • What the lease says
  • Which expenses are recoverable
  • Which expenses remain with the owner
  • What happens during vacancy
  • Whether current recoveries match actual expenditure
  • Whether future costs may emerge

Potential categories can include

  • Council rates
  • Water charges
  • Body corporate / strata costs
  • Insurance
  • Maintenance
  • Statutory expenses
  • Management costs
  • Repairs and capital items
Lease expiry

THE LEASE ENDS. WHAT HAPPENS next?

Reletting is a process with both cost and time implications. Beyond the vacancy itself, it can involve make-good, works to prepare the property for another tenant, professional fees, advertising, leasing commission, incentives, rent-free periods, downtime and owner-funded outgoings.

  2. Make-good / works

Liquidity

How much cash should you keep after settlement?

Putting every available dollar into the acquisition can leave an owner exposed when the unexpected occurs.

This is a risk-management question, not financial advice. There is no universal cash-reserve figure. Individual circumstances vary, and financial advice should come from appropriately qualified advisers.

Potential future cash requirements can include

  • Vacancy
  • Repairs
  • Capital expenditure
  • Insurance
  • Unrecovered outgoings
  • Leasing costs
  • Tenant incentives
  • Due diligence costs
  • Finance commitments

The acquisition price isn't necessarily the last cheque you'll write.

From residential to commercial

Moving from residential property into commercial?

Commercial property can operate very differently from residential investment. The opportunity can also be different.

The answer is not to avoid commercial property.

It is to understand the investment before buying it.

Differences may include

  • Longer lease structures
  • Tenant-funded outgoings
  • Larger vacancy periods
  • Incentives
  • Make-good
  • Specialised buildings
  • Business covenant risk
  • GST considerations
  • Different finance structures
  • Greater due diligence requirements

Common questions

Buying commercial property

What should I check before buying a commercial property?

Check the property, the tenant, the lease, the income, the outgoings, the likely capital expenditure, the market the property competes in and how easily it could be relet or sold. Also identify what information has not yet been verified. Legal, building, tax and finance matters should be reviewed by the relevant specialist advisers.

What happens if a commercial tenant goes broke?

Rent may stop, and the owner may need to fund outgoings and finance commitments while the property is relet. What can be recovered depends on the lease, any guarantees or security held and the tenant's circumstances. Legal advice is needed on the owner's rights in a specific case.

What happens if a commercial property becomes vacant?

Rental income stops and the owner generally carries the outgoings that a tenant would otherwise have paid. Reletting can also involve advertising, leasing commission, incentives and works to prepare the space for a new tenant.

How long can a commercial property stay vacant?

There is no standard vacancy period. It depends on the property, its location, its suitability for likely tenants, the asking rent, the incentives offered and market conditions at the time. Buyers should understand how the property would perform if the income stopped, rather than assume a particular period.

Who pays outgoings on a commercial property?

It depends on the lease. Many commercial leases allow some or all outgoings to be recovered from the tenant, but some costs may remain with the owner, and during vacancy the owner generally pays them. The lease wording and the applicable legislation determine what is recoverable.

What are commercial property outgoings?

Outgoings are the ongoing costs of owning and operating a property. They can include council rates, water charges, body corporate or strata levies, insurance, maintenance, statutory expenses and management costs.

What should I look for in a commercial lease before buying?

Key items include the remaining term, options, the rent review mechanism, recoverable outgoings, incentives, guarantees and security, repair and maintenance obligations, make-good provisions, assignment provisions, permitted use and what happens at expiry. The lease should be reviewed by your solicitor.

What is tenant covenant quality?

Covenant quality is essentially the strength and reliability of the tenant and the lease supporting the property's income. It is a judgement drawn from several factors, including the tenant entity, trading history where available, guarantees, security and the lease terms, rather than a single score.

What happens when a commercial lease expires?

The tenant may renew, exercise an option if one exists, or leave. If the tenant leaves, the owner may face make-good negotiations, works, marketing, a period of vacancy, leasing costs and incentives before income restarts.

What is make-good in a commercial lease?

Make-good is a tenant's obligation, where the lease includes one, to return the premises to an agreed condition at the end of the lease. What it requires, and whether it is enforced or settled, depends on the lease wording and the circumstances.

How much cash should I keep after buying a commercial property?

There is no universal figure. It depends on the property, the lease, the tenant, finance arrangements and your wider financial position. Allowing for vacancy, repairs, capital expenditure, unrecovered outgoings and leasing costs is prudent, and a qualified financial adviser should help set the amount.

What due diligence should I do before buying commercial property?

Typical due diligence covers the lease and contract, tenancy and income, outgoings, building condition, services and compliance, planning and permitted use, and market evidence for rent and value. The scope depends on the property, and specialist advisers are usually needed for legal, building, planning, tax and finance matters.

Do I need a commercial buyer's agent?

Not every buyer does. A buyer's agent can be useful if you are new to commercial property, short on time, or want someone exclusively representing your interests while the selling agent represents the vendor.

What does a commercial buyer's agent do?

A commercial buyer's agent acts for the buyer. The work can include defining the acquisition brief, sourcing and screening properties, assessing tenants, leases and income, coordinating due diligence with specialist advisers and negotiating the purchase.
