Buying Business Premises

How do I avoid buying the wrong premises for my business?

A building can look right on inspection and still be the wrong purchase for the business. The harder questions are what it will cost to operate from, whether the timing works with your current lease and what happens if the business outgrows it. This guide sets out what I would test before you commit.

Direct answer

How do I avoid buying the wrong premises for my business?

Start with the business, not the listing. Define how the business operates, what it needs from a building now and over the next several years, and what it can afford to spend in total. Then test each property against that brief: operational fit, the full cost of occupation, timing against your current lease, planning and permitted use, and how easily the property could be adapted, leased or sold later.

A good price does not make the wrong building suitable. Each requirement needs evidence, and anything that cannot yet be verified should be treated as an unknown rather than an assumption.

The question is not only: “Is this a good price?”

It is: “Will this building work for the business, and what will it really cost us?”

Start with the business, not the building

Buying premises is a business decision before it is a property decision. The building will shape how the business operates, what it costs to run and how much capital is tied up in it for years.

Before inspecting anything, write down what the business needs: floor area, site area, layout, access, power, parking, location for staff and customers, budget and timing. Separate the requirements that cannot move from the preferences that can. That brief becomes the test every property is measured against.

The risks then fall under six headings: operations, total cost, timing, planning and compliance, the building itself and future flexibility.

01

Operational fit

Test the building against how the business actually works, not how it looks on the day.

  • Access and loading. Truck access, turning circles, hardstand, roller door heights and dock arrangements for the vehicles the business uses.
  • Clearance and layout. Internal height, column spacing, office-to-warehouse ratio and whether the floor plan suits the workflow.
  • Power and services. Available electrical capacity, three-phase supply, data and any services specific to the operation.
  • Parking and location. Staff and customer parking, public transport and travel time for the people the business relies on.
  • Growth capacity. Whether the site allows for expansion, or whether the business is likely to outgrow it within the period it expects to own it.

02

The total cost of occupation

The purchase price is only part of what the business will spend. Before agreeing a price, estimate the full cost of getting into the building and operating from it.

  • Acquisition costs. Transfer duty, legal fees, due diligence and finance costs.
  • Adaptation and fit-out. Works needed before the business can operate, including power upgrades, office fit-out, racking and compliance works.
  • Outgoings. Rates, insurance, maintenance and other costs the business carries as owner.
  • Capital expenditure. Roof, structure, services, plant and compliance items the building is likely to need over the ownership period.
  • Opportunity cost. Capital tied up in the property is capital not available to the business.

Compare owning with leasing using the lease vs buy calculator, and compare premises with the occupancy cost comparison.

03

Timing and your current lease

Most businesses buying premises are leaving somewhere else. The purchase has to work with that exit.

Check when the property is available with vacant possession, whether any existing occupier has rights to stay, and how settlement and fit-out timing align with your current lease expiry, options and make-good obligations. A gap or an overlap can cost the business rent, double occupancy or disruption to trading.

If the timing is uncertain, it may be worth reviewing your current lease first. Your options there may be stronger than they look.

04

Planning, permitted use and compliance

A building that suits the business physically may not be approved for the use. Zoning, existing approvals, permitted use, flood and overlay constraints, and building compliance all need to be confirmed before you are committed.

These matters should be investigated by your solicitor, town planner and building consultant. Our role is to identify the questions early and make sure they are answered before the contract becomes unconditional.

05

The building itself

Condition matters more to an owner-occupier than many buyers expect, because the business carries every repair. Ask for building condition information, maintenance records and compliance certificates, and commission a building inspection appropriate to the property.

I would want the real cost of the building understood before the price is agreed, not discovered after the business has moved in.

06

Future flexibility

Businesses change. Before buying, consider what happens if the business grows, shrinks, relocates or is sold.

A building that suits a wide range of occupiers can be leased or sold more easily if your plans change. A highly specialised building, a secondary location or an unusual configuration narrows that pool. That is not always a reason to walk away, but it is a risk to weigh in the price.

The hardest questions are often the ones you don't know to ask

Buyers are often given a great deal of information about a property. Having information is not the same as knowing whether the building suits the business. The problem is often not knowing which information matters.

What I would look at before buying

Before recommending a purchase, I want each of these answered with evidence, or recorded as an assumption or an unknown to be verified.

  1. Brief. Does the property meet every requirement the business cannot compromise on?
  2. Operations. Will the access, layout, power and parking work for how the business operates?
  3. Total cost. What will the business spend to buy, adapt and occupy the building?
  4. Timing. Does settlement and fit-out align with your current lease?
  5. Planning. Is the intended use approved, or approvable, at this site?
  6. Building. What expenditure is likely over the ownership period?
  7. Flexibility. Could the property be leased or sold if the business's plans change?
  8. Unknowns. What has not yet been verified?

Common questions

What should I check before buying premises for my business?

Check whether the building suits how the business operates, the full cost of buying and occupying it, how the timing aligns with your current lease, whether the intended use is permitted, the condition of the building and how easily it could be leased or sold later. Legal, planning, building, tax and finance matters should be reviewed by the relevant specialist advisers.

Is it better to lease or buy business premises?

It depends on the business, its capital, how long it expects to stay and how certain its space needs are. Buying gives control and can build equity, but ties up capital and leaves the business carrying the building's costs and risks. Leasing keeps capital in the business and offers more flexibility. The lease vs buy calculator compares the two.

What costs are involved in buying business premises?

Beyond the price, costs can include transfer duty, legal and due diligence fees, finance costs, adaptation and fit-out, rates, insurance, maintenance and future capital expenditure on the building. Your accountant and solicitor should confirm the costs that apply to your purchase.

How do I time a purchase with my current lease?

Check your lease expiry, any options and your make-good obligations, then compare them with the property's vacant possession date, settlement and the time needed for fit-out. A gap or overlap can be costly, so the timing should be planned before the contract is signed.

Can my SMSF buy premises for my business?

In some circumstances a self-managed super fund can buy business real property and lease it to a related business, subject to strict rules. This needs advice from your accountant and a licensed financial adviser before you proceed. The SMSF business premises calculator provides general estimates only.

Do I need a buyer's agent to buy business premises?

Not every buyer does. A buyer's agent can be useful if you are short on time, new to buying commercial property, or want someone exclusively representing the business while the selling agent represents the vendor. The work can include defining the brief, screening properties, assessing suitability and cost, coordinating due diligence and negotiating the purchase.
