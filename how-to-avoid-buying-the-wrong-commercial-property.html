How do I avoid buying the wrong commercial property?

A commercial property can look attractive on the day you buy it. The harder question is what happens afterwards: if the tenant fails, if the property sits vacant or if you need to sell. This guide sets out what I would test before committing capital.

Direct answer

How do I avoid buying the wrong commercial property?

Understand what you are acquiring before you commit capital. For a tenanted investment, that means the property, the tenant, the lease, the income, the outgoings and what happens if the income stops. For an owner-occupier, it also means whether the building works for the business and who would want it later.

A headline yield or current rent does not explain those risks. Each needs evidence, and anything that cannot yet be verified should be treated as an unknown rather than an assumption.

The question is not only: “What does this property yield today?”

It is: “What happens to me financially if the assumptions change?”

You're buying more than the building

A tenanted commercial investment is not only land and improvements. It is an income stream whose quality depends on the tenant, the lease, the property, the market and the obligations the owner takes on. An owner-occupier purchase carries a different version of the same problem: the building must suit the business now and appeal to someone else later.

The risks fall under five headings: the property, the income, the tenant and lease, the downside and the exit. Each should be answered with evidence, or recorded as an assumption or an unknown to be verified.

01

The property

Start with physical suitability. Does the building work for its current use, and would it work for the likely alternative users in that location? Clearance, access, loading, parking, power, configuration and site coverage decide how wide that pool of users is.

Flexibility matters because tenants and businesses change. A building that can be subdivided, reconfigured or adapted keeps more options open than one designed around a single operation. A highly specialised building can suit one occupier very well and very few others.

Then look ahead. Roofs, structure, services, plant, compliance and presentation all carry future capital expenditure. The purchase price is rarely the last cheque an owner writes.

02

The income

An advertised yield is a calculation, not a fact. It depends on what rent is included, which outgoings are deducted and what assumptions sit behind both.

  • Current rent against market rent. A rent above market may not survive the next review or renewal. A rent below market may be an opportunity, or a sign of weakness.
  • Incentives. A face rent supported by a large incentive overstates the effective income.
  • Outgoings and recoverability. “Net lease” does not mean every expense is recoverable. The lease wording, the legislation and actual expenditure decide what the owner pays.
  • Advertised yield assumptions. Check whether the figure uses gross or net income, whether vacancy is allowed for and whether costs the owner will carry have been left out.

03

The tenant and the lease

A lease is only as useful as the tenant's ability and willingness to perform it. Covenant quality is the strength and reliability of the tenant and the lease supporting the income. It is a judgement drawn from several factors, including the tenant entity, trading history where available, guarantees and security. It cannot be reduced to a single score.

Then read what the lease actually says. A summary in an Information Memorandum is not a substitute. Key items include:

  • Remaining term, options and expiry dates
  • The rent review mechanism
  • Recoverable outgoings and the tenant's other financial obligations
  • Incentives still to be paid
  • Guarantees and security
  • Repair, maintenance and make-good obligations
  • Assignment provisions and permitted use

For buyers moving from residential property, this is where the largest gaps usually sit, because commercial leases work very differently from residential tenancies. The lease and contract should be reviewed by your solicitor. Our role is to identify the commercial issues and what should be investigated with the right advisers.

04

The downside

The central question is not only what the property yields today. It is what happens to you financially if the income stops.

Tenant failure

If a tenant fails, rent may stop while the owner continues to fund outgoings and finance. What can be recovered depends on the lease, any guarantees or security held and the tenant's circumstances.

Vacancy and reletting

When a tenant leaves, income restarts only after make-good, any works, marketing, a period of vacancy, the new lease negotiation and any incentive. Each step costs time, money or both. There is no standard vacancy period. It depends on the property, the location, the asking rent and the market at the time. Nine months is not a normal expectation, but it is a useful test of whether the investment still works.

Liquidity buffer

Putting every available dollar into the purchase leaves an owner exposed when vacancy, repairs, unrecovered outgoings or leasing costs arrive. There is no universal cash-reserve figure. It depends on the property, the lease, the finance and your wider position, and should be set with a qualified financial adviser.

I would want the downside understood before the price is agreed, not discovered after settlement.

05

The exit

Every purchase is also a future sale. Before buying, consider who is likely to want the property when you sell, and whether that pool is wide or narrow.

Relettability drives much of the answer. A property that suits many tenants holds its appeal when a lease ends. A specialised building, a secondary location or an unusual configuration narrows the buyer pool and can take longer to sell.

Ask what happens if the original investment thesis changes. If the tenant leaves, rents soften or your own plans change, could the property be relet, adapted to another use or sold without a large discount? If the answer depends on a single tenant or a single type of buyer, that is a risk worth pricing.

The hardest questions are often the ones you don't know to ask

Buyers are often given a great deal of information. An Information Memorandum may set out the rent, lease expiry, outgoings, building area and advertised yield. Having information is not the same as understanding the acquisition. The problem is often not knowing which information matters.

The selling agent represents the vendor, which is exactly their role. It does mean the vendor has a professional working towards the vendor's objective. A buyer committing significant capital should decide whether they also want someone representing their side. What I would look at before buying

Before recommending a purchase, I want each of these answered with evidence, or recorded as an assumption or an unknown to be verified.

  1. Property. Is the asset suitable, functional and relettable to more than one type of user?
  2. Income. Is the rent sustainable against the market, after incentives and unrecovered outgoings?
  3. Tenant and lease. Who pays the rent, and what rights, obligations and future events does the lease contain?
  4. Downside. What happens if the tenant leaves or fails, and can the buyer absorb the vacancy?
  5. Capital. What expenditure is likely over the holding period?
  6. Exit. Who is likely to want the property when it is sold?
  7. Unknowns. What has not yet been verified?

Common questions

What should I check before buying a commercial property?

Check the property, the tenant, the lease, the income, the outgoings, the likely capital expenditure, the market the property competes in and how easily it could be relet or sold. Also identify what information has not yet been verified. Legal, building, tax and finance matters should be reviewed by the relevant specialist advisers.

What happens if a commercial tenant goes broke?

Rent may stop, and the owner may need to fund outgoings and finance commitments while the property is relet. What can be recovered depends on the lease, any guarantees or security held and the tenant's circumstances. Legal advice is needed on the owner's rights in a specific case.

What happens if a commercial property becomes vacant?

Rental income stops and the owner generally carries the outgoings that a tenant would otherwise have paid. Reletting can also involve advertising, leasing commission, incentives and works to prepare the space for a new tenant.

Who pays outgoings on a commercial property?

It depends on the lease. Many commercial leases allow some or all outgoings to be recovered from the tenant, but some costs may remain with the owner, and during vacancy the owner generally pays them. The lease wording and the applicable legislation determine what is recoverable.

What should I look for in a commercial lease before buying?

Key items include the remaining term, options, the rent review mechanism, recoverable outgoings, incentives, guarantees and security, repair and maintenance obligations, make-good provisions, assignment provisions, permitted use and what happens at expiry. The lease should be reviewed by your solicitor.

Do I need a commercial buyer's agent?

Not every buyer does. A buyer's agent can be useful if you are new to commercial property, short on time, or want someone exclusively representing your interests while the selling agent represents the vendor. The work can include defining the brief, screening properties, assessing tenants and leases, coordinating due diligence and negotiating the purchase.
