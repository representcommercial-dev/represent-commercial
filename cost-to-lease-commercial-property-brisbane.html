Commercial Property Leasing

How much does it cost to lease out a commercial property?

Leasing a commercial property can involve commission, advertising, photography, floor plans and incentives before rent starts again. Those costs matter, but the larger cost is often vacancy. This article sets out what each cost covers and what to check before spending more.

Direct answer

What does it cost to lease a commercial property?

There is no single fixed cost to lease a commercial property. Owners may incur leasing commission, advertising, professional photography, floor plans, incentives and other campaign expenses. The total cost also includes the financial effect of vacancy, such as lost rent, unrecovered outgoings and ongoing holding costs.

The appropriate amount depends on the property, market, lease terms and leasing strategy.

The question is not simply: “Why does it cost so much to lease my property?”

It is: “What is the total cost of getting the property leased well?”

Where does the money go?

Most leasing costs fall into five categories. What is appropriate depends on the property, the market and the tenants it is likely to attract.

Leasing commission

Commercial leasing agents generally charge a fee when a new lease is successfully negotiated. How that fee is calculated varies between agency appointments, so there is no single standard rate. Confirm in writing what the commission covers, how it is calculated and when it becomes payable.

Advertising

Advertising is usually an owner cost, agreed as part of the agency appointment. Commercial portals offer different packages and levels of exposure. Wider exposure can help, but a larger package does not fix problems with pricing, presentation or positioning. Approve a marketing budget before the campaign starts.

Photography

Photographs should help a tenant understand the actual space on offer. Generic, outdated or poorly chosen images create uncertainty, and uncertainty gives a tenant a reason to move on to the next listing.

Floor plans and property information

Dimensions, floor plans, access, parking, outgoings and practical property information help a tenant decide whether an inspection is worth making. Missing information is one of the most common problems, and one of the least expensive to fix.

Incentives

Depending on the property and market, a lease may involve rent-free periods, fitout contributions or other incentives. An incentive reduces the effective rent, so weigh it against the face rent and against the cost of a longer vacancy. The effective rent calculator shows the trade-off.

The cost owners easily overlook

Every week of unnecessary vacancy costs the rent that is not being received. It also costs outgoings the owner cannot recover from a tenant, ongoing holding costs such as finance, and sometimes further marketing.

On a property that would lease for $1,000 a week, eight additional weeks of avoidable vacancy is $8,000 of foregone rent before unrecovered outgoings are counted. A modest difference between two agents' fees can matter much less than that.

The cheapest agent isn't necessarily the cheapest outcome

Owners should compare leasing commissions and management fees. Price alone does not show whether a leasing strategy represents good value. The aim is not to minimise the agent's fee. It is to secure the right tenant, on the right terms, without unnecessary downtime.

A lease is an asset decision. It affects the property's income and value for years after the campaign ends.

Why isn't the property leasing?

When a property is slow to lease, the usual responses are to reduce the rent, spend more on advertising or change agents. Each can be right. Each can also be money spent on the wrong problem. The first step is finding where the friction sits.

Asking rent and competing stock

Is the asking rent supported by the market, and by what a tenant can lease nearby? The comparison should include the incentives competing properties are offering, not what this property achieved last time.

Presentation and information

Does the listing communicate the property's advantages? Do the photographs help? Can a tenant work out the likely occupancy cost, or is important information missing?

Enquiries and inspections

How quickly are enquiries answered, and how easy is it to arrange an inspection? Slow responses lose tenants who are comparing several properties at once.

Inspection feedback

What are tenants saying after they inspect, and has that feedback changed the campaign, the presentation or the terms? Feedback is evidence, and it is often left unused.

Changing agents

Changing agents helps when the campaign has a problem the current agent is not addressing. It does not help if the underlying issue is the rent, the property or the market.

What I would look at

Before recommending that an owner spend more on advertising, reduce the rent or change agents, I would want to understand where the friction sits.

  1. Is the rent right for this property now? Compared with competing properties and the incentives they offer.
  2. What does a tenant see first? The listing, the photographs, the floor plan and the information that decides whether to inspect.
  3. Can a tenant work out the real cost? Whether outgoings and the likely occupancy cost are clear.
  4. What happens after an enquiry? Response time, inspection access and follow-up.
  5. What is the feedback telling us? And whether it has changed anything.
  6. Is the spend in the right place? Which problem more advertising or a lower rent is meant to solve.

Common questions

How much do commercial leasing agents charge?

Commercial leasing agents generally charge a fee when a new lease is successfully negotiated. How that fee is calculated varies between agency appointments, so there is no single standard rate. Owners should confirm in writing what the commission covers, how it is calculated and when it becomes payable.

Who pays to advertise a commercial property for lease?

Advertising is usually an owner cost, agreed as part of the agency appointment. The amount and what it includes depend on the campaign agreed between the owner and the agent, so owners should approve a marketing budget before the campaign starts.

How much does commercial property vacancy cost?

Vacancy costs the rent that is not being received, plus outgoings the owner cannot recover from a tenant and other holding costs such as finance. The amount depends on the rent, the outgoings and how long the property stays vacant. Each additional week of unnecessary vacancy adds to it.

Should I spend more advertising a vacant commercial property?

Sometimes. More exposure can help, but a larger advertising package does not solve problems with asking rent, presentation, missing information or slow responses to enquiry. It is worth identifying why the property is not leasing before increasing the budget.

Why isn't my commercial property leasing?

Common causes include an asking rent the market does not support, stronger competing stock, a listing that does not communicate the property's advantages, missing property or occupancy-cost information, and friction in answering enquiries or arranging inspections. Each requires a different response.

Should I change commercial leasing agents?

Not necessarily. Changing agents makes sense if the campaign has a problem the current agent is not addressing. It does not help if the underlying issue is the rent, the property or the market. An independent review can help separate those questions before you decide.
